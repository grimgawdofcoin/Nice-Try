Don't trade the break.
Trade the continuation ♥

ICC reads every market move as one repeating cycle. The higher timeframes (4H · 1H) hand you the direction. The lower timeframes (15M · 5M · 1M) hand you the entry. Everything in between is waiting — and the waiting is the edge.

I

Indication

Price breaks a previous major high or low — a break of structure signaling intent and a possible new leg.

no trade. The first break is often a liquidity grab built to trap breakout traders.

Correction

The counter-move. Price pulls back into a pre-marked Area of Interest — prior supply/demand — sweeping early entrants' stops.

no trade mid-pullback. Let it reach the AOI and show exhaustion.
C

Continuation

The strike zone. Correction stalls at the AOI, a confirmation candle prints on a low timeframe, and price resumes — you enter with the flow.

Enter only on a confirmed resume — never on hope.

Timeframe correlation

4H · 1H — direction

  • Mark structure: higher-highs + higher-lows = bullish; lower-highs + lower-lows = bearish.
  • Draw AOIs here — zones where buyers and sellers clearly separated.
  • Never fight the HTF: a "correction" on the 4H is a whole trend on the 5M.

15M · 5M · 1M — entry

  • Time the trigger inside the HTF AOI — engulfing, hammer/shooting star, micro double bottom/top.
  • A valid swing needs at least two confirming candles.
  • Price must break and close beyond the prior swing to confirm the turn.

Entry · Stop · Target

Long — bullish continuation

  • Enter on the LTF higher-low + reclaim inside the AOI.
  • Stop below the correction low — never inside the correction zone.
  • Targets: TP1 = 1R (partials, stop → breakeven) · TP2 = prior high · TP3 = new structure high.

Short — bearish continuation

  • Enter on the LTF lower-high + rejection inside the AOI.
  • Stop above the correction high — never inside the correction zone.
  • Targets: TP1 = 1R · TP2 = prior low · TP3 = new structure low. Aim ≥ 2:1.

Risk discipline

Guardrails

  • Risk ~1–1.5% per session · cap at 2 signals per session.
  • Stop to breakeven at 1R. Never widen a stop.
  • One trade per direction per session. Done after 2 losses in a day.

The four oopsies

  • Trading the Indication / first break.
  • Entering mid-Correction before the AOI.
  • Ignoring the higher-timeframe bias.
  • Overtrading C-grade setups · revenge trading.

FOR BRAND-NEW TRADERS ♥

What am I even looking at?

No experience needed. This page explains every shape and color on the screen, then lets you practice the one skill everything else is built on: reading the bias.

A price chart is just a picture of a fight between buyers and sellers over time. Each little shape is one round of that fight. Once you can read a single shape — and which way the crowd is leaning — the rest of the method clicks into place. Let's start with the shape.

1 · The candle

Every candle is one slice of time. The fat part is the body — the gap between where price opened and closed in that slice. The thin threads are wicks, marking the highest and lowest price reached before it settled.

Color tells you who won the slice:

Up — closed higher than it opened Down — closed lower than it opened

2 · Timeframes — zoom levels

Each candle = a chunk of time

  • 4H — one candle is 4 hours. The big picture.
  • 1H — one candle is 1 hour.
  • 15M — one candle is 15 minutes.
  • 5M / 1M — fine detail, down to one minute per candle.

The golden rule

  • Direction comes from the big timeframes (4H, 1H). They tell you which way the river is flowing.
  • Entry comes from the small ones (15M, 5M, 1M). They tell you the exact moment to step in.
  • Same market, different zoom — like a map vs. street view.

3 · Bias — which way is the crowd leaning?

Bias is the single most important read in trading: is the market climbing, or falling? The trick is to watch the peaks and valleys. Climbing peaks & climbing valleys = up. Sinking peaks & sinking valleys = down. Practice it with both tools below — one shows you, one tests you.

4 · The gold band & the blue line

Gold band = Area of Interest (AOI)

  • A zone where price reacted strongly before — lots of buyers or sellers waiting.
  • When price returns there, it often bounces or stalls. It's where the good setups live.
  • Think of it as a doorway price has to pass through.

Blue line = market structure

  • It connects the swing highs and lows so the staircase is easy to see.
  • Up-staircase = bullish bias. Down-staircase = bearish bias.
  • When the staircase suddenly breaks the other way, the bias may be flipping.

5 · The whole method in one breath

Indication = the move that hints at a direction — not yet a pattern, often a trap.  Correction = the pullback against that move — wait, it's still forming.  Continuation = price resuming the original direction from the gold band — this is the completed pattern. Big timeframes set the direction; small timeframes show the detail. That's ICC.

7 · Tiny glossary

⚖️Bias

The market's leaning — bullish (up) or bearish (down).

🏔️Swing high / low

A local peak or valley on the chart — the steps of the staircase.

📏Support / resistance

Price floors and ceilings where moves tend to pause or reverse.

💧Liquidity

Clusters of resting orders (and stop-losses) that price likes to hunt.

🎣Sweep / stop hunt

A quick poke past a level to grab those orders, then a reversal.

🚀Displacement

A big, fast candle showing real intent behind a move.

🔍HTF / LTF

Higher timeframe (bias) vs. lower timeframe (entry).

🎯R (risk:reward)

Reward measured in units of risk. 2R = you aim to make twice what you'd lose.

㉑️Double bottom / top

Two lows (or highs) at the same level. A double bottom (W) is a bullish reversal; a double top (M) is bearish. The shared level is the zone that matters.

👤Head & shoulders

Three peaks: a higher middle (head) between two lower shoulders. A close below the neckline signals a reversal. Flip it upside-down for the bullish version.

🔁Support → resistance flip

When price breaks a level, it often flips roles: old support becomes new resistance (and vice-versa). The retest of a flipped level is a classic AOI.

8 · Find the AOI yourself

The gold band isn't magic — you can mark it yourself. An AOI is just the launchpad: the little base of candles a strong move took off from. Big orders were resting there, so when price returns it often reacts. Learn to spot the launchpad and you'll never need anyone to draw the zone for you.

How to find it — 5 steps

  • Zoom out to a higher timeframe (4H / 1H) — AOIs are drawn on the big picture.
  • Spot a strong, fast move — a big run of candles one direction.
  • Find where it started: the quiet base right before the run. That base is your zone.
  • Took off up → demand (buyers). Took off down → supply (sellers).
  • Best zones match your bias and are fresh — price hasn't returned to them yet.

The one trap to avoid

  • The AOI is where price ran FROM, not where it ran TO.
  • The top of a big rally is exhaustion — not a demand zone.
  • Always mark the origin of the move: the calm base before the explosion.

9 · Hold or break? — the hardest live read

Finding a zone is half the skill. The half that decides correction vs reversal is what happens when price returns to it. Does it hold (bounce, trend continues) or break (close through, trend flips)? Your tell is the dashed structure line: if price closes beyond it, the bias is broken. This is the exact read that turns a dip into a "buy" instead of a panic sell.

10 · Reading indicators

Those wiggly lines below price — RSI and MACD — measure momentum, not direction. They're a second opinion, never the decision.

RSI

A 0–100 momentum gauge. Above ~70 = overbought (stretched up), below ~30 = oversold (stretched down). New price high but a lower RSI high = divergence — momentum quietly fading.

MACD

Two moving-average lines plus a histogram. Lines crossing up = momentum turning up; down = turning down. Bars growing = momentum building behind the move.

Golden rule: indicators confirm, they don't lead. Read structure and the AOI first, then let RSI/MACD agree or warn — never let them override the chart. A bullish chart with cooling RSI is still bullish.

11 · When timeframes disagree

The trap that catches everyone: the 1-hour can be bullish while the daily is bearish. Both are true — they're different clocks. A rally on the 1H is often just a bounce inside a bigger downtrend.

Which clock owns your decision?

  • Pick your trading timeframe first (say 1H) — that sets the bias you act on.
  • Glance one step up (4H / daily) for context: are you trading with the bigger trend, or against it?
  • With the bigger trend = higher-probability, hold longer. Against it = smaller target, faster exit.

The mistake to avoid

  • Don't use a daily feeling to call a 1-minute move — or the reverse.
  • A correction on the daily can be an entire trend on the 5-minute.
  • When two timeframes conflict, the higher one usually wins out eventually.
Real example: a coin up 9% today (1H bullish) can still be down 40% on the year (daily bearish). The pop is real — but it's a counter-trend bounce. Trade it smaller, and don't mistake it for a brand-new uptrend.

12 · Common mistakes

Almost every red day traces back to one of these. Tap each to see the fix.

🏃Chasing the green candle

Buying the breakout spike instead of waiting for the pullback into the zone it launched from. The retest is the entry, not the spike — chasing means a worse price and a wider stop.

🔪Catching a falling knife

Buying a drop before it reaches an AOI or shows any turn — and price just keeps falling. Wait for the zone plus a reaction, not just "it looks cheap."

🔄Correction vs reversal

Reading a normal pullback as a trend change. Until structure breaks (a close beyond the prior swing), a dip in an uptrend is just a dip — not a reason to flip bearish.

🎰Trading the first break

Jumping on the first push through a level — often a liquidity grab that snaps back. Let it sweep, reclaim, and retest before you commit.

📉Ignoring the higher timeframe

Going long on a 1H bounce inside a daily downtrend. Always check the bigger clock first — context decides whether your setup is with the trend or fighting it.

💸Revenge trading

Forcing a trade right after a loss to "win it back." That's emotion, not a setup. Step away after two losses — the market will still be here tomorrow.

14 · ICT & smart-money concepts

ICC is a simplified cousin of ICT (Inner Circle Trader). The vocabulary differs, but the engine is the same idea seen through an institutional lens: price hunts liquidity, then moves with intent from specific zones. Here's the deeper layer.

💧Liquidity (buy-side / sell-side)

Clusters of stop orders resting above swing highs (buy-side) and below swing lows (sell-side). Big players push price into these pools to fill size. Equal highs/lows are liquidity magnets.

🧤Liquidity sweep / stop hunt

Price spikes just beyond a high/low to trigger stops, then snaps back. The sweep + reclaim is often the real entry — the move begins after liquidity is taken, not before.

🧱Order block

The last opposite-colour candle before a strong displacement move — where institutions loaded their position. Price often returns to it before continuing. This is ICC's AOI by another name.

⛽Fair value gap (imbalance)

A three-candle gap where price moved so fast it left an untraded void (candle 1's high to candle 3's low). Markets tend to revisit and "fill" it. A fresh FVG is a high-quality AOI.

🔀BOS vs CHoCH

Break of structure = trend continues (price takes the next high in an uptrend). Change of character = first break against the trend (an uptrend's higher-low fails) — the early reversal warning. This is the precise version of "structure broke."

⚖️Premium / discount & OTE

Split any range at 50%. Buy in the discount (lower) half, sell in the premium (upper) half. The OTE (optimal trade entry, ~62–79% retrace) is the sweet spot for joining the trend cheaply.

🕑Killzones / sessions

Volatility concentrates in session windows (London open, New York open). ICT times entries to these "killzones" because that's when liquidity and displacement actually happen — context for when, not just where.

🎭Judas swing

A false move early in a session that traps traders the wrong way before the real move reverses. The fake-out is engineered to grab liquidity — recognising it keeps you from chasing.

How it maps to ICC: the AOI is an order block, the strong "indication" move is displacement (often leaving an FVG), the correction is a retrace into discount/premium, and "structure broke" is a CHoCH. Same skeleton, richer language.

15 · The power of indicators

This cuts against pure price action — but used well, indicators add a real edge by measuring things your eyes can't: momentum, participation, and where the average position sits. The mistake is overloading. Pick two or three that disagree with each other and read them as confluence.

📐Moving averages

Trend filter and dynamic support/resistance. Price above a rising 50/200 = bullish regime. Crossovers (fast over slow) flag momentum shifts; the 200 is the line institutions watch.

🌀RSI — beyond overbought

The real power is divergence: price makes a new high, RSI makes a lower high → momentum is fading before price turns. Also, in strong trends RSI staying "overbought" is strength, not a sell.

📊MACD

Momentum and trend in one. The line cross signals shifts; a growing histogram confirms a move has force behind it. Best as a second opinion on a structure-based entry.

🔊Volume

The truth serum. A breakout on high volume is real; on low volume it's suspect. Climax volume at extremes often marks exhaustion. Volume confirms whether order flow backed the candle.

⚓VWAP

Volume-weighted average price — the day's "fair value" anchor that institutions and algos trade around. Price above VWAP = buyers in control; reclaims and rejections of it are high-quality intraday levels.

🎈Bollinger Bands

Volatility envelope. A "squeeze" (bands pinching) precedes big moves; tags of the outer band in a range mark stretch. Pairs well with RSI for mean-reversion reads.

Golden rule, restated: stack confluence, don't clutter. Structure/AOI tells you where; a momentum tool (RSI/MACD) and a participation tool (volume/VWAP) tell you whether to trust it. Two agreeing signals beat six conflicting ones.

16 · Reading order flow

Candles are the summary; order flow is the cause. It's the live tug-of-war of buy and sell orders that actually moves price. You don't need it to trade ICC — but understanding it is what turns "the zone held" from a rule into a reason. This is the deepest layer most retail traders never reach.

📒The order book (DOM)

A live ladder of resting bids (buy limits below price) and asks (sell limits above). It shows where liquidity sits. Price can only move by consuming orders on the book.

⚔️Market vs limit orders

Market orders (takers) cross the spread and move price by eating liquidity. Limit orders (makers) sit and wait, providing liquidity. Price moves when aggressive takers overwhelm passive makers on one side.

↔️Spread & liquidity

The gap between best bid and best ask. Tight spread + deep book = liquid, orderly. Thin book = fast, gappy moves. Big moves happen where liquidity is thin and stops are clustered.

🧿Absorption

Heavy market selling hits the book but price won't drop — a big passive buyer is absorbing every sell. That hidden demand is exactly why an AOI holds. Absorption at a level often precedes the bounce.

⛔Imbalance / stacked orders

Far more aggression on one side (e.g., buyers lifting every offer) with little resistance. Stacked buying = price gets pulled up fast. This aggression is what creates displacement and FVGs.

📈Delta & CVD

Delta = buying volume minus selling volume per bar. CVD (cumulative delta) sums it over time. Rising price + falling CVD = a move with no real buying behind it (a warning); price up and CVD up = healthy.

👣Footprint charts

Each candle broken down by buy vs sell volume at every price. You see exactly where aggression happened, where absorption stalled a move, and where a candle was hollow — the X-ray of a bar.

🎯Why it matters

Price isn't random — it's drawn toward resting liquidity (stops, large limits) and moves when one side gets aggressive. Order flow is the why beneath every ICC beat: displacement is aggression, an AOI holds on absorption, and a sweep is liquidity being collected.

The honest caveat: full order-flow tools (DOM, footprint, CVD) suit fast intraday and futures trading and carry a real learning curve. You can trade ICC well without them — but knowing the mechanism makes your zone reads less like faith and more like cause-and-effect.
